Focus groups

Focus Groups, Start to Finish

The screener, the discussion guide, the topline report and everything else a focus group project produces, shown on one fictional frozen pizza study.

A focus group is a moderated conversation with six to ten people who share something that matters to the question. They buy the category, they buy it often or rarely, they live in a market you care about. Ninety minutes, one moderator, and a room next door where the client team watches. It is one of the oldest tools in qualitative research and still one of the most useful, when it is used for the right job.

What question it answers

Groups answer why and how, not how many. They are good at finding the words people use for a category, the reasons a concept lands or falls flat, and the objections nobody on the team thought of. They are poor at sizing anything. If seven of eight people in a room pick the same concept, that is a reason to test it with a few hundred people, not a forecast.

How we run it

Every project starts with the screener, because the right room matters more than the right questions. We write terminate and quota logic so each group is distinct: heavy buyers are not mixed with light buyers, and people who work in the industry or sat in a group recently are screened out. We recruit ten to seat eight. The discussion guide comes next, with timed sections and written probes, so every group covers the same ground and the moderator still has room to follow something unexpected. Concept boards and package mockups are rotated so no idea always goes first. The client team watches live, and a short back room check before the wrap-up makes sure the last minutes go where the team needs them.

Quiet ballots, before and after

In-person groups carry a bias of their own. Once one confident person speaks, the rest of the room tends to drift toward them, and a hand vote twenty minutes later records the drift as much as the opinion. We offset that with a short evaluation built into the discussion guide. Before any conversation about a concept or product, each person rates it on paper, on their own and in quiet. At the end of the session they rate it again. It interrupts the flow of the group for a few minutes, and it is often the most revealing part of the night: you see what people thought before the group got to them, which ideas the conversation strengthened, and which ones it talked people out of.

What you get

Speaker-labeled transcripts within two business days of each group, full recordings, a highlight reel cut by theme, and a topline report a few days after the last group, walked through in a live debrief. A full report with a quote bank follows. Kickoff to topline usually takes about three weeks, and recruiting is the step most worth protecting.

Where it goes wrong

Most failed groups fail in recruiting. A loose screener fills the room with practiced respondents or people who barely use the category, and the conversation drifts. The second failure is reading a hand vote as a market share. The third is one confident voice steering the room. A good moderator draws out the quiet participants, and the quiet ballots show how far the room moved anyone. And a group cannot show you what someone does alone in a grocery aisle. It tells you what they say about it, which is useful, and different.
