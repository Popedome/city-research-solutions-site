Tidewell is weighing its current can against two redesigns. 450 sparkling water buyers each saw one design only, 150 per design. First task: find Tidewell Pink Grapefruit on a simulated shelf of 12 products, with a 20 second limit. We recorded whether they found it, how long it took, and what they clicked by mistake. The test can was rotated across four shelf positions.

All 450 respondents rated ten candidate on-pack claims, shown in random order, on two scales: how appealing each one is, and how believable. Scores are the share choosing the top two boxes of a five point scale. A reach analysis (often called TURF) then asks a different question: which small set of claims gives the most people at least one claim they find appealing?

Reach as claims are added Modeled

Reach is the share of respondents who find at least one claim in the set appealing. The top three by appeal mostly reach the same people: the clean-label buyer. The best set adds a flavor claim and a hydration claim that reach different buyers.

Three ways to pick three claims Modeled

The recommendation brings the three tasks together. Every row in the scorecard is measured on 150 people per design, and the last two columns say whether a gap is large enough to trust. Significant means 95% confidence on a two-sample test of proportions or medians. Directional means it could be noise.

The recommendation

Move to Shoreline.
Lead with zero sugar.

Shoreline is found more often and faster than the current can, is mistaken for a competitor about a third as often, and is the design whose brand people remember [shelf and heat map, measured]. Fruit Forward is liked slightly more, but the gap is within noise and it costs 22 points of brand recall. On pack, run zero sugar, electrolytes and extra-fine bubbles [claims, reach modeled].
