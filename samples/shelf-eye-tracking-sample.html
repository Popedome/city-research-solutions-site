Tidewell wants to know whether a new shelf layout would help it at retail. We built two full-size sparkling water sets in our facility: the current set, where Tidewell sits at the right end of the aisle, and an alternative set that moves it to the center at eye and waist level. 40 category shoppers wore eye-tracking glasses, 20 per set. Each looked at the shelf freely for 10 seconds, then shopped it and chose the one 8-pack they would buy.

How the test works

FormatIn person, central location. Two real shelf sets built side by side in the facility.
Who40 adults 21 to 54 who bought sparkling water in the past month. Randomly assigned to one set.
EquipmentLightweight eye-tracking glasses, calibrated for each shopper before they reach the aisle.
Task10 seconds of free viewing, then shop the set and take the one 8-pack you would buy.
MeasuresWho looked at each pack, time to first look, number of looks, total looking time, what was bought.
OutputsHeat maps, spotlight maps, package-level scores, a side-by-side read of the two sets.

Who took part measured

Quotas kept age, gender and buying frequency matched across the two sets.

Each map combines every glance from the first 10 seconds the 20 shoppers spent in front of the set. Heat map shows where looking piled up. Spotlight darkens everything nobody looked at. The two count views show how many of the 20 looked at each pack, and what they finally bought.

Six of eight Tidewell packs make the alternative top 10. None of them made the current top 10 except Lime. At the right end of the aisle, half of Tidewell sat on the top and bottom shelves, where few shoppers looked in the first 10 seconds.

The whole shelf, not just Tidewell. Did the alternative layout make the category easier to shop, or did Tidewell only borrow attention from its neighbors?

Read the size of these gaps as directional. With 20 shoppers per set, the eye-tracking measures show where attention moves and why. To size the effect, we pair this test with an online shelf test of a few hundred shoppers.

What changed across the whole set

Shoppers looked at slightly more packages, found their first one a little sooner and stayed longer on the packs they looked at. The gains are modest for the category as a whole and large for Tidewell, which means the alternative layout mostly redistributes attention toward the center of the aisle rather than creating a lot of new looking. Corrowan, which held the left end of eye level in the current set, is still the second brand but is no longer the most looked at.

Recommendation

Take the alternative layout to the retailer. Moving Tidewell from the right end of the aisle to the center, at eye and waist level, roughly doubles the number of shoppers who look at each Tidewell pack (2.9 to 5.9 of 20), gets the first look about 1.4 seconds sooner and lifts Tidewell from 3 to 8 of 20 purchases.

The category does not lose. Shoppers look at slightly more packs overall and stay longer on each one. Average price paid dips 2.3%, because Tidewell sells below the category leader. Raise that with the buyer before they raise it with you.

Next step: confirm the purchase shift with an online shelf test of about 300 shoppers per layout, and test whether Tidewell keeps most of its gain with only the eye-level row, since center waist-level space is harder to win.

Why in person

Glasses record where people actually look at a real shelf, at real distance, with real lighting and real neighbors. It catches what a screen cannot: packs above or below the line of sight, ends of the aisle, and how people move along a set.

Why online too

Online heat maps reach hundreds of shoppers in days, at a fraction of the cost. They are the right tool for comparing package designs and sizing an effect. In person is the right tool for layouts, fixtures and displays.
